About

I am a Lecturer at Edith Cowan University, with an Adjunct Fellowship at Curtin University and an Honorary Research Fellowship at the University of Queensland.

My research investigates the neural and cognitive mechanisms of motor learning, with a focus on dopaminergic modulation and non-invasive brain stimulation. I am also interested in application of these approaches to inform the design of rehabilitation and training interventions in Parkinson’s disease, healthy ageing, and neurodevelopmental conditions.

Alongside my research, I coordinate undergraduate and graduate-diploma psychology units and supervise honours and higher-degree research students.

Research

Aim 1

Mechanisms of motor learning

I test how errors and rewards shape motor memories, using sensorimotor adaptation. Earlier work separated sensory prediction errors from reward prediction errors; later work showed that motor memories can take both a goal-directed and an associative form.

Current work examines how reward and dopamine drive skill learning in young and older adults, and how temporal and action prediction develop together.

Aim 2

Neuromodulation of function

I use non-invasive brain stimulation, pharmacology, exercise and auditory stimulation to probe causal brain mechanisms and to inform treatment. Work on rhythmic auditory cueing shows that individuals respond differently to the same beat.

Recent studies show that the response to brain stimulation depends on brain dopamine levels, which matters for ageing, depression, and Parkinson’s disease.

Teaching

Philosophy

I treat critical thinking as the point of a university education, not a by-product of it. My role as a teacher is to help students judge scientific evidence for themselves and to communicate their reasoning clearly.

I teach critical thinking explicitly, through scaffolded assessment and deliberate practice. Students rehearse the moves that experts make — dissecting an argument, weighing evidence, writing with precision — with structured guidance, worked exemplars and frequent feedback. Difficult material is made accessible through concrete analogies, so that students can engage with the complexity rather than retreat from it.

“Teaching critical thinking instead of just regurgitating course content … quite grounding compared to the abstraction from textbook-based lecture content.” Student feedback

I design for access. Students arrive with different needs, including vision and hearing disabilities, acquired brain injuries, learning disabilities, and varied cultural backgrounds. I tailor communication, materials and engagement norms so that each student has a fair route into the content.

I revise my teaching from year to year in response to student feedback and outcome data. What worked once is not assumed to work again; what did not work is redesigned.
